# Competitive Analysis

A comparison is only useful if it is fair, current, and built on the criteria the buyer
actually uses. Flattering analysis loses deals; honest analysis wins the right ones.

## Scope

- Define the question: a sales battlecard, a pricing decision, a product roadmap input,
  or a market overview. The question decides which criteria matter.
- Name the alternatives, including "do nothing", in-house solutions, and spreadsheets.
- Choose five to eight buyer criteria (capability, price model, ease of adoption,
  integrations, support, security, fit for segment), ideally from lost and won deals.

## Evidence

- Use legitimate sources only: public websites, documentation, pricing pages, published
  reviews, analyst reports you have rights to, and your own customers' firsthand
  accounts. Never obtain competitor material through deception or confidentiality breach.
- Record for each fact the source and the date seen. Mark anything older than six
  months, or second-hand, as needing a recheck.
- Separate facts ("offers a free tier up to 3 users", source, date) from opinions and
  anecdotes ("a customer said setup was slow").
- Say "unknown" instead of guessing a competitor's capability or price.

## Analysis

1. Rate each alternative per criterion: stronger, comparable, weaker, or unknown,
   relative to your offering, with the evidence.
2. Identify where you win, where you lose, and where the buyer cannot tell the difference.
3. For each loss area decide: counter (a real advantage elsewhere), concede (not the
   right customer), or fix (product input with the deal evidence).
4. Write sales responses to the common objections that are true and checkable.

## Output

```text
Question: <purpose> | Segment: <buyer type> | As of: <date>
Criterion     | Us        | Competitor A              | Competitor B
Pricing model | per user  | per usage (pricing page, May) | unknown
Integrations  | 40 native | 12 native (docs, May)     | 60+ (website, April)
Win where: <criteria + evidence> | Lose where: <criteria + evidence>
Positioning: <one or two sentences>
Objection "<X is cheaper>": <true, checkable response>
Recheck: <facts older than six months or unverified>
```

## Common failure modes

- Comparing your roadmap with their current product.
- Treating one angry review as a competitor's typical performance.
- Battlecards that disparage competitors with claims that cannot be substantiated.
